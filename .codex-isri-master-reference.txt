03 The problem
Voice is fast becoming the default interface to the digital world — voice assistants, dictation, voice
notes, and now agentic, voice-driven browsing. For millions of people, that shift is not liberation; it is
a new wall.
Commercial automatic speech recognition (ASR) relies on a single universal model trained
overwhelmingly on typical, fluent, accent-neutral speech. Non-standard speech is, by contrast, 
high-variability and low-resource: there is little training data, and collecting it is physically effortful
for the very people who need it most. A one-size-fits-all model will always fail at the tails of the
distribution — and the tails are exactly where our users live. For many of them, mainstream speech
recognition fails more often than it succeeds.
Who is affected: people with cerebral palsy, Down syndrome, stammers/stutters, stroke- and injury-
related speech changes, Parkinson's disease, and deaf accents, among others — tens of millions of
people worldwide, and a very large, under-served population across Africa.
What exclusion looks like in daily life:
- Voice notes that go unsent, out of fear of being misunderstood.
- Phone calls and voice interfaces avoided entirely.
- Voice assistants and dictation tools that simply fail, forcing dependence on caregivers or family for
basic digital tasks.

04 Our insight and approach
You cannot solve non-standard speech with one bigger model. You solve it one person at a time.
Echo's core thesis is personalization. Instead of forcing a person to match a "standard" voice, we
build a model that learns their voice. The user records a set of phrases; Echo automatically trains a
lightweight, personalized adapter that learns that individual's pronunciation, rhythm, and articulation;
and every downstream interaction runs through a model tuned specifically to them.
This is simultaneously more accurate and more respectful. It is more accurate because a model
specialized to one speaker vastly outperforms a generic model on that speaker. It is more respectful
because we are not asking anyone to sound "correct" — we are building technology that has learned
to understand them as they are.
Personalized low-rank adaptation for impaired speech is an active international research frontier.
ISRI's distinction is making it work as a shipped, real-world product — for African speakers and
languages that global efforts overlook — rather than as a paper.

06 The Echo Engine (deep dive)
The Echo Engine is the personalization pipeline that powers everything.
How it works, end to end:
1. Enrolment. A user records a set of phrases — between roughly 100 and 1,000 utterances (on the
order of ten minutes of speech for a usable model). Enrolment is designed to be low-burden,
because speaking is effortful for many of our users.
2. Adapter training. Echo automatically trains a custom LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) adapter for
that individual on top of a strong ASR base (a Whisper-family model). The adapter is small — on the
order of a few megabytes — and learns the person's specific pronunciation, rhythm, and articulation.
We built a custom LoRA-injection architecture to make this work reliably where off-the-shelf PEFT
tooling did not.
3. Storage and serving. Each adapter is stored and served through an API, so any Echo product can
call it to transcribe or understand that specific person's speech accurately.
4. Production pipeline. Transcription can be followed by an LLM correction layer (cleaning the
transcript) and, where wanted, voice reconstruction that renders the output in the speaker's own
voice — their words, their voice, just clearer.
Infrastructure: GPU training on Modal (A100-class), with an S3 / Modal / DynamoDB pipeline for
data, training, storage, and inference. This is a live production system, not a prototype.
Results: In internal experiments, personalized adapters have reduced word error rate by up to ~42%
relative (over 23 percentage points absolute) for individual cerebral-palsy speakers versus the non-
personalized baseline. Every user adapter trained also feeds back into improving the base model, so
accuracy compounds over time.

07 The Echo App
The Echo App is our first live product — the first thing powered by the Echo Engine, and the one in
pilot today. It gives an individual user direct access to their own personalized model through a set of
tools:
Transcription — accurate speech-to-text on the user's own voice.
Dictation studio — capturing, organizing, and drafting longer thoughts by voice.
Voice cloning — reproducing the user's own voice for expressive output.
Voice-note export & sharing — clean audio and transcripts exported into the messaging, email,
work, and school platforms users already rely on, so Echo becomes a bridge into their existing
life.
Saved recordings — a personal library the user can revisit and reuse.
The Echo App is where users experience, for the first time, being able to speak naturally and be
understood — without correction, repetition, or the fear of being misheard.
08 The product suite
With the engine and app live, ISRI is building three products for the coming quarter, all on the same
foundation.
8.1 Echo Tutor — voice-first learning (starting with mathematics)
What it is: a learning platform that lets students with disabilities — dysgraphia, cerebral palsy, motor
impairments, non-standard speech — do mathematics by speaking rather than writing.
The key innovation: most educational tools evaluate answers; Echo Tutor evaluates thinking. It
follows a three-stage, voice-first workflow that mirrors how learners actually reason:
1. Free thinking & brainstorming — the student talks through what they notice, possible strategies,
and relevant ideas, with no judgement or correction. The goal is to capture raw mathematical
thinking without the burden of writing.
2. Guided reflection — Echo Tutor asks open-ended questions (What is the problem asking? What
do you already know? What could be your first step? How would you check your answer?) that turn
scattered ideas into a structured plan.
3. Guided solution building — the student walks through each step verbally; the platform

8.2 Lekezi — confidence-building speech practice
What it is: a mobile app that helps people with speech impairments (cerebral palsy, Down syndrome,
deaf accents, and more) practise the specific things they most want to say — and see concrete
evidence that their delivery is getting stronger.
What it deliberately does NOT do: it does not try to change how a person sounds, and it does not do
phonetic correction. It optimizes for the two things that can meaningfully change — confidence and 
capability on specific real-world goals (saying a name, placing an order, giving an introduction).
How it works — the reference-text loop: every exercise has a reference phrase the user is aiming to
say. Echo transcribes their attempt; an LLM layer compares "what they meant to say" against "what
they actually said" and turns the gap into warm, specific, behavioural coaching ("four weeks ago you
hesitated on 'cappuccino' every time; now you finish the whole order cleanly in four of five tries;
your filler words dropped from six to two").
Five core features: goal-based practice modules; difficulty progression / scaffolding (single word →
phrase → full scenario, with transfer tracking); instant behavioural feedback; repetition tracking and
progress visualization; emotional check-ins and confidence self-ratings.
The measurement insight: because Echo's models are personalized, transcription accuracy rises as
the model learns the user — which measures the model improving, not the person. Lekezi is
designed from the ground up to avoid this trap: it measures person-centred signals (filler counts,
completion time, stumbles, pauses, completion rate, clean-run streaks, self-rated confidence), never
model accuracy, as evidence of human progress.
Positioning: a complement to speech therapy, never a replacement — it brings the practice half of
therapy home, affordably, in the long stretches between scarce and costly sessions. Built with
therapists and NGOs co-designing the scenario library. Build horizon: a focused three-month plan to
a working pilot.

accessibility assistant that understands them, not a generic voice.

09 Where we are: pilot, history, and traction
We are in active pilot testing.
Version 1 (2025). We fine-tuned a Whisper model on non-standard Kenyan Swahili speech data,
collected in partnership with the Maria Njeri Foundation, with GPU support from Modal. This proved a
fine-tuned model could understand voices mainstream systems could not — but it was a single
shared model, not personalized. We tested it with 10 users, processing hundreds of recordings in
production.
Version 2 (2026 — current). We cracked per-user personalization — the leap that turns "better than
nothing" into "understands you." We are now piloting with a deliberately small, diverse cohort of 5
users, capped at a maximum of 10 for this phase. This constraint is a choice, not a limitation we're
hiding: without funding yet, we are prioritizing depth over scale — intensive, personal support for
each tester so we deeply understand their needs and build the right things next. We collect as much
feedback as possible and iterate on it directly. Funding and support would let us scale this while
keeping the quality of support high.
The pilot cohort is intentionally diverse, so Echo is stress-tested across genuinely different acoustic
and human profiles. Illustrative testers:
- A young man with a severe stammer, from a low-income background — for whom being
understood is also a route to opportunity he has been shut out of.
- A user with mild speech differences from cerebral palsy.
- An 11-year-old who is deaf, fitted with a cochlear implant at age five, with resulting speech
differences.
Traction and validation to date:
- A working, personalized ASR engine — the hard technical problem — solved and running in
production (up to ~42% relative WER reduction in internal tests).
- A live Echo App in real users' hands.

10 Technology and stack
Base ASR: Whisper-family models, adapted per user.
Personalization: custom LoRA-injection architecture producing small (~few-MB) per-user
adapters from ~100–1,000 utterances; solves off-the-shelf PEFT/Whisper incompatibilities.
Language intelligence layer: an LLM sits above the ASR for transcript correction, reasoning,
coaching feedback (Lekezi), reflective tutoring (Echo Tutor), and intent/agentic action
(VoiceFlow). This layer is model-flexible — it currently runs on a leading LLM and can be pointed
at whichever model best fits a given deployment or funder (e.g. Gemini, Claude), including open
models (e.g. Gemma) for on-device, offline, privacy-preserving use in low-connectivity settings.
Voice output: optional voice-reconstruction so output can be rendered in the speaker's own
voice.
Infrastructure: Modal (A100-class GPU training), S3, DynamoDB; adapters stored and served via
API. Live production system.
Roadmap direction: scaling the personalization and inference pipeline for many concurrent
users, and moving toward on-device deployment for offline, private use.
Note for funder-specific applications: where a programme requires a particular model family to be
materially load-bearing (e.g. Google's Gemini/Gemma/Veo), position the intelligence layer on that
family — Gemini for reasoning and agentic action across all three products, Gemma for on-device
personalized deployment. The architecture is designed to accommodate this.

13 Business model and sustainability
ISRI is a social enterprise: mission-first, with a trading revenue model designed so that commercial
income funds reach into the communities that need it most.
Revenue paths:
- Institutional / B2B & B2NGO: healthcare providers, schools, disability organizations, and therapists

mandates.

Dual-entity structure: ISRI UK Limited anchors international funding, partnerships, and governance;
the Kenyan entity delivers on the ground. This structure supports both grant/impact funding and
commercial trading revenue, and lets us pursue UK- and Africa-facing opportunities in parallel.
